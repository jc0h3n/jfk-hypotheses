# Method

## Why ACH here

The course presents each thesis through an advocate (the syllabus: each theory is "presented by Attorney Sheehan as an ADVOCATE of that Thesis") and asks students to challenge each one before choosing their own. ACH is built for exactly that: instead of collecting support for a favorite explanation, it rates every piece of evidence against every hypothesis and favors the one with the *least evidence against it*. Evidence consistent with everything tells you nothing; what matters is evidence that discriminates.

Twining's reading ("Evidence as a multi-disciplinary subject," 2003) makes the same point from law: what unifies work on evidence across law, intelligence analysis and history is inferential reasoning, judging what a piece of evidence actually supports, how strongly, and through which chain of inferences.

## How the evidence was chosen

- **Both sides, deliberately.** The syllabus and both chronologies lean toward conspiracy explanations and draw mainly on three pro-conspiracy books (Fonzi, Douglass, Hancock). David Reitzes's *Skeptic* article argues the other side. Evidence was taken from both, plus the official record (Warren Commission, HSCA, Church Committee, the National Academy of Sciences acoustics review, the Assassination Records Review Board).
- **Evidence, not conclusions.** Items describe what was found, said or recorded, and who said it, rather than what it proves. Where an item has a well-known counter-explanation, the counter-explanation is in its notes, not hidden.
- **Claims are labeled as claims.** An allegation reported in the course materials is entered as a Report or an Argument with its source, never as a Fact.
- **Not exhaustive.** The Extended Chronology alone runs to 275 pages. This is a starting set built around the syllabus's 20 Key Questions; add to it.

## Credibility and relevance

These are starting points, set as follows. Change them where you judge differently; Open ACH weights the scores by them.

**Credibility** (can this item be relied on as stated?)

- **High:** physical or documentary evidence, or findings examined by more than one independent body; or a fact both sides accept.
- **Medium:** a single official finding that is contested; consistent eyewitness or expert testimony; a well-documented secondary account.
- **Low:** uncorroborated, single-sourced or second-hand claims; accounts given long after the event or later recanted; an advocate's inference.

**Relevance** (if true, how much does it bear on the question?)

- **High:** directly bears on who fired, or who directed it.
- **Medium:** bears on motive, capability, opportunity or the reliability of other evidence.
- **Low:** context, or answers a side question.

## Things to watch while rating

- **Consistent with everything = not diagnostic.** Oswald's Marxism, for example, may be consistent with several hypotheses; ask what each hypothesis predicts you would see.
- **Rate against the hypothesis, not the theorist.** A theory's advocates may explain away an item; the question is whether the hypothesis itself leads you to expect it.
- **Absence of evidence counts**, especially after 60 years and millions of released pages: ask whether each hypothesis predicts that something should have surfaced by now.
- **Withholding is ambiguous.** Agencies hid things (plots against Castro, the Hosty note). Concealment fits both a cover-up of involvement and the protection of unrelated operations or embarrassment.
- **Sensitivity.** When you're done, Open ACH's sensitivity check shows which items your leading hypothesis hinges on. Those are the ones to verify against primary sources before your thesis rests on them.
