The Kennedy assassination, tested with Analysis of Competing Hypotheses · by jc0h3n

Sixty years of theories about the assassination of President Kennedy, tested the way intelligence analysts test competing explanations: list every plausible hypothesis, rate each piece of evidence against all of them, and favor the hypothesis with the least evidence against it, not the one with the most evidence for it.

There are two analyses below. (A) Who fired the shots? weighs the physical, medical, photographic, acoustic and eyewitness evidence. (B) Who was behind it? weighs seven explanations, from Oswald alone to organized crime, the CIA, Cuba, the KGB, the military and a broader coalition. Every item cites its source, and well-known counter-explanations are in its notes. The facts behind it are in the case library: a searchable timeline, the ten theses and sixty other theories, the cast of characters, the 20 key questions, the readings, CIA cryptonyms and a book list.         How ACH works

        Analysis of Competing Hypotheses was developed by Richards J. Heuer Jr. at the CIA in the 1970s to counter a common trap: settling on the first plausible explanation and then noticing mostly the evidence that fits it. ACH makes you consider every reasonable explanation at once, and judge them by the evidence against each one.

        1. Hypotheses. List every plausible explanation, including ones you think are unlikely and ones involving deception. A group brainstorm works well here.
        2. Evidence. List the evidence and arguments that bear on the question. Include your assumptions, and the absence of things you'd expect to see.
        3. Matrix. Rate each item against every hypothesis by working across a row, not down a column. Ask: if this hypothesis were true, how likely is it I'd see this?
        4. Refine. Reword or combine hypotheses as you learn more. Set aside evidence that's consistent with everything, because it doesn't help you choose.
        5. Tentative conclusions. Look for the hypothesis with the least evidence against it. Try to disprove hypotheses rather than prove them.
        6. Sensitivity. Ask which few items your conclusion depends on. What if they're wrong, misread, or planted? Check their sources.
        7. Report. Discuss the relative likelihood of every hypothesis, not just the winner, so readers see what was considered and rejected.
        8. Milestones. Note what you'd expect to see if your conclusion is wrong, and watch for it.

        Ratings

        How the scores work

        Weighted inconsistency. Each Inconsistent rating counts 1 point against a hypothesis and each Very inconsistent rating counts 2. Each point is multiplied by the evidence's credibility and relevance (Low 0.5, Medium 1, High 1.5), so a highly credible, highly relevant item that's very inconsistent counts 4.5. Lower is better. Consistent ratings don't add points, because evidence that fits several hypotheses doesn't help you choose between them.

        Diagnosticity measures how much an item's ratings differ across hypotheses. Ratings are scored from Very consistent (+2) to Very inconsistent (−2); the spread between the highest and lowest is 0 for "not diagnostic" and 3 or more for "high."

        Sensitivity removes each item of evidence in turn and checks whether a different hypothesis would lead. Items that change the leader are flagged as critical.

        The scores are an aid to judgment, not a verdict. Heuer's advice was to use the matrix to see your reasoning clearly, then decide for yourself.
